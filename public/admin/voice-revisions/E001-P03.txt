E001-P03 — Seventeen

The siren disappeared first.

One moment it was somewhere beyond the rain and traffic, rising and falling while a young woman kept telling Flavio to stay with her. The next, there was only the uneven clicking of an electric fan. He drew in a breath so sharply that his chest hurt, then froze, waiting for the old pressure to close around his ribs again. It did not. There was no blood in his mouth, no rain against his face, no pavement beneath him. Air filled his lungs from top to bottom so easily that for several seconds he did nothing but breathe, slowly at first, then deeper, as though his body had forgotten what the act was supposed to feel like.

Above him was a ceiling. Not sky, not headlights, not the impossible green world of the Genesis advertisement that had burned through the last of his vision. A ceiling. There was a hairline crack near one corner, thin enough to look like somebody had dragged a pencil through the plaster and stopped halfway to the light fixture. Beneath it, the fan turned with a faint wobble, clicking three times before falling quiet for part of a rotation and beginning again.

Flavio stared at it longer than made sense. He knew that sound. He had once spent an entire night listening to that same clicking before an examination, convinced that the crack in the ceiling meant the plaster was about to fall on him. Teresa had climbed onto a chair the next morning, pressed one hand against it, declared the house still capable of surviving another day, and sent him back to his books. He had forgotten the argument completely until now.

The room smelled of warm dust, clean sheets, old wood, and something cooking beyond the door. Morning light entered through an open window and stretched across the floor. Outside, a motorcycle passed through the lane, followed by a neighbor calling a greeting to someone farther down. The answer came back with the lazy familiarity of people who had probably repeated the same exchange for years. Everything sounded ordinary. That was the frightening part.

Flavio slowly raised one hand into view.

The hand was wrong.

His fingers were thinner, the skin along his knuckles smoother, and the small marks left by years of work were gone. The thumbnail he remembered splitting against a metal shelf looked untouched. He flexed his fingers once, then again. They moved without pain, stiffness, or trembling.

He sat up too quickly and had to grab the mattress when the room shifted slightly around him. The blanket slid into his lap, exposing thin legs and arms that had not yet spent eighteen more years lifting, carrying, fixing, sorting, and doing whatever else had been necessary to keep his family moving. The shirt hanging from his shoulders looked familiar in a way that made his stomach tighten. Teresa had bought it for him when he was young. He turned one wrist over and searched for a faded scar beneath the bone.

Nothing.

“Where am I?”

His own voice startled him. It was lighter, younger, rough with sleep in a way he had not heard in years.

The question was wrong anyway. He knew where he was. He wanted to know when.

Flavio swung his feet onto the floor. The tiles were cool, and one slipper waited farther beneath the bed than the other. On the small desk sat notebooks, loose papers, a plastic cup full of pens, and a folded sheet with softened corners. He knew all of them. Some had been thrown away long before he died. Others had disappeared during one of the moves he preferred not to remember.

He picked up a notebook and opened it. His own handwriting stared back at him, smaller, impatient, crowded in places where he had tried to save paper. There were corrections in the margin and calculations written too quickly to be neat. One word had been pressed hard enough into the page to leave an impression on the next sheet. Flavio ran his thumb over it, then closed the notebook before the simple familiarity of his own handwriting became harder to bear than the impossible date he had not yet checked.

Across the room, the cracked mirror on the wardrobe gave him his first clear answer.

A boy stared back at him.

Flavio did not recognize himself immediately, not because the face was unfamiliar, but because he had spent too many years remembering it incorrectly. At seventeen, he had never thought he looked young. He had thought he looked tired, poor, too thin, and behind other people his age in every way that seemed important. From thirty-five, the boy in the mirror looked unfinished.

There were no permanent shadows beneath his eyes yet. His face was narrower. His hair had survived the night by declaring war on gravity. He touched one cheek and watched the reflection do the same.

Seventeen.

The thought came quietly, but it landed hard.

Flavio turned away from the mirror and searched the desk for his phone. The model itself made his chest tighten. Old. Not old here, but old to him. His fingers remembered the unlock pattern before he consciously did, and when the screen came alive, he went directly to the date.

He stared.

Then checked the calendar app.

Then the settings.

Then the paper calendar beside the door.

Same day, same month, same year!

Flavio looked from the phone to the calendar and back again, as though repetition might eventually force the numbers to correct themselves. They did not. A grocery reminder had been written in one corner in Teresa’s handwriting, the letters leaning slightly to the right.

Buy cooking oil.

He touched the ink with his thumb.

That ordinary note hurt more than the date.

A year before Genesis launched.

He remembered that much. He had not cared enough about the game in his first life to count down to release, but Isabel had. Nico had talked about it too, and later, when Genesis became too large to ignore, even people who had never played knew what it was. By the time Flavio died, giant advertisements covered buildings.

One of them had been the last thing he saw.

The woman in the rain returned to him in fragments—wet hair, a face he had never managed to see clearly, a warm hand wrapped around his, and the same words repeated while everything else disappeared.

Stay with me.

Flavio lowered the phone.

No enormous screen stood beyond the window now. No city intersection. No rain.

Just the narrow lane outside the house, sunlight catching on corrugated roofs, a neighbor carrying a plastic bag, someone sweeping water away from a doorway.

Genesis had not launched yet.

He sat on the edge of the bed and looked toward the photograph tucked beside the mirror. He already knew what was in it, but he pulled it free anyway. Teresa, Isabel, and Flavio stood outside the house, all three squinting into sunlight. His mother looked younger. Isabel looked irritated by the camera. Flavio looked like smiling had been an unreasonable request.

Rogelio was not there.

The absence struck harder than he expected.

For one brief, foolish second, some part of him had hoped that if time itself had broken open, it might have opened a little farther. Fourteen instead of seventeen. Thirteen. Before his father died. Before the house became something they remembered instead of something they came home to.

But Rogelio was still gone.

The room could return. The date could return. His mother and sister could still be somewhere beyond the door.

His father remained outside the reach of this miracle.

From the kitchen came the sharp sound of a spoon striking the rim of a pot.

Flavio stopped moving.

A cupboard opened, then shut. Someone crossed the kitchen floor with the slow, familiar rhythm of a person who knew where every object belonged. He had heard those footsteps almost every morning of his childhood without ever understanding that one day he would give nearly anything to hear them again.

He stood, then sat back down.

Teresa.

She should be in the kitchen.

Alive.

Younger.

Probably annoyed that he had not gotten out of bed yet.

He stared at the bedroom door.

Checking a calendar had been easy. So had inspecting scars, comparing phone models, and looking through notebooks. Objects could be wrong. A room could be a dream. A photograph could be memory rearranging itself.

A voice was different.

“Flavio?”

His entire body went still.

Teresa’s voice came through the door, younger than the one he remembered from the end of his life, but unmistakably hers.

“Flavio? You’re awake?”

Flavio tried to answer and failed the first time. His throat tightened before the sound came.

“I’m awake.”

His voice cracked on the second word.

There was a brief pause, then the handle turned.

Teresa pushed the door open with one hand while balancing a plate in the other. Her hair had been tied back loosely, with several strands already escaping near her temple. There were lines around her eyes, but fewer than he remembered. Her shoulders were straighter. Her hands did not tremble.

Flavio forgot the calendar.

Forgot the phone.

Forgot Genesis.

His mother stood in the doorway.

Teresa looked at his bare feet first.

“You’ll get cold standing there.”

Of all the things she could have said, it had to be that.

Flavio nearly laughed. The sound rose halfway and caught somewhere behind his ribs, turning into something dangerously close to a sob. He looked down before she could read too much from his face.

Teresa’s expression changed.

“What’s wrong?”

He shook his head too quickly.

“Nothing.”

She set the plate on the desk and studied him more carefully.

“You look pale. Are you feeling sick?”

Flavio wanted to say no, but thirty-five years of memory stood between him and the woman in front of him. He remembered hospital visits that had not happened yet, medicine she had not begun taking, days when she would try to hide discomfort because he already had too much to worry about. None of those years had reached her yet.

Teresa stepped closer and placed her palm against his forehead.

Flavio almost broke.

The touch was ordinary, warm, steady. He remembered that same hand shaking while she opened medicine packets, and how often he had pretended not to notice.

“No fever,” she said.

Flavio closed his eyes for half a second.

Her palm remained there.

“Ma.”

Teresa drew back slightly.

“What?”

He had too many answers.

I’m sorry. I died. I missed you. You got sick. I worked too much. I should have come home earlier. I should have—

“Nothing,” he said at last. “I just had a bad dream.”

Teresa’s eyes narrowed.

“What kind of dream?”

Flavio looked at her face and, because apparently death had not improved his judgment, answered with the first thing that came to mind.

“You were old.”

Teresa stared at him.

“How old?”

“Very.”

She slapped his shoulder lightly.

Flavio laughed.

This time the laugh escaped properly, awkward and unsteady, but real enough that Teresa looked even more suspicious.

“That dream damaged your manners,” she said.

“Sorry.”

“You should be.”

She pointed toward the plate.

“Eat before your sister decides your breakfast has become public property.”

Isabel.

Flavio’s breath caught again.

Teresa noticed immediately.

“What now?”

“Isabel’s here?”

His mother stared at him for several seconds, and Flavio could almost see her reconsidering the fever diagnosis.

“Where else would she be?”

Right.

Of course.

This morning was impossible only to him.

To Teresa, it was breakfast. To Isabel, probably another school day. To the neighbors outside, just another morning in the lane.

Flavio looked down at his hands.

His mother was alive. His sister was somewhere beyond the door. His father was still dead. Genesis had not launched.

And Flavio Reyes was seventeen again.

Teresa touched his shoulder, not dramatically, just enough to pull his attention back.

“You really are strange this morning.”

“I know.”

“Eat.”

He nodded.

Teresa reached the doorway, then glanced back.

“If the dream was that bad, you can tell me after breakfast.”

Flavio looked at her.

In his other life there had always been something before conversations like that. Work, bills, medicine, another shift, exhaustion, tomorrow.

He picked up the plate.

“Okay.”

Teresa gave him one last doubtful look and left the door partly open.

The house returned through that opening. A chair scraped across the kitchen floor, a cupboard closed, and somewhere beyond the hallway Isabel complained about something he could not quite hear. Teresa answered with the patient irritation of a mother who had probably heard the complaint before.

Flavio remained beside the bed for another moment, holding the plate with both hands.

He looked at the calendar, the photograph, his young fingers curled around the edge of the plate, then toward the open door.

He still did not know why he was here. He did not know what had happened after he died, who the woman in the rain had been, or whether the future he remembered would unfold the same way again.

For once, he let the questions remain unanswered.

His mother was in the kitchen.

His sister was waiting somewhere beyond the hallway.

Breakfast was getting cold.

Flavio stood and carried the plate toward them.

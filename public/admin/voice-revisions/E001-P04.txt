E001-P04 — The House He Lost Once

Flavio stepped through the open doorway carrying the plate in both hands, and for several seconds he forgot to move any farther.

The kitchen was smaller than he remembered.

That was the first thing that struck him, not because anything had changed, but because memory had stretched the room over the years. In his mind, the old house had become larger whenever he thought about it, as if everything he had lost needed more space to remain important. Now he stood inside it again and saw the truth: the narrow passage beside the table, the low shelf crowded with containers, the rice cooker pushed too close to the wall, and a plastic basin tucked beneath a chair because there had never been enough storage.

Morning had already settled into the room. Steam clung to the lid of a pot on the stove. The smell of garlic, rice, and something fried lingered in the air, mixing with the faint scent of detergent from clothes drying somewhere near the back. Sunlight entered through the window above the sink and caught the scratches on the table, every pale line suddenly visible.

Flavio knew some of those scratches.

One near the corner had come from a school project Isabel finished directly on the table despite being told not to. Another had appeared when Rogelio repaired something there late one evening and dragged a tool across the surface. Teresa had complained for three days afterward, though she never actually replaced the table.

He had forgotten the scratches before today.

He had remembered the house. He had remembered losing it. He had remembered boxes, debts, moving things from one place to another, and the feeling of locking a door that no longer belonged to them.

But not the scratches.

A spoon struck a bowl.

Flavio looked toward the table.

Isabel sat there with one knee tucked beneath her, her hair still untidy from sleep and an open notebook beside her breakfast. She was writing something with the expression of someone being personally insulted by mathematics.

For a moment, Flavio could only stare.

She was younger.

Of course she was younger, but knowing it and seeing it were two different things.

The sister he remembered most clearly was the woman who had learned to stop asking him whether he would come home early. She had gotten good at pretending his absence did not bother her. She would send a message, wait, and then send another only if it was important. By the end, she had become careful around his schedule in a way no younger sister should have needed to be.

This Isabel had no such discipline.

She looked up, caught him staring, and immediately frowned.

“What?”

Flavio almost laughed.

There it was.

No hesitation. No careful reading of his face. No polite decision about whether she was interrupting him.

Just what?

He sat across from her.

“Nothing.”

Isabel narrowed her eyes.

“You’re weird today.”

“Apparently.”

She looked toward the kitchen, where Teresa was putting away a container.

“Ma, Kuya is being strange.”

Teresa did not turn around.

“I noticed.”

Flavio lowered his head and finally took a bite of breakfast.

The food was hot enough to burn his tongue.

He nearly smiled anyway.

Teresa cooked the same way he remembered, which meant seasoning by instinct, measuring by habit, and treating recipes as suggestions for people who did not know what they were doing. Flavio had eaten thousands of meals like this growing up and complained about some of them.

He would have paid anything for one yesterday.

Yesterday.

The word settled strangely inside him.

Technically, yesterday had been eighteen years from now.

He stopped chewing.

Isabel noticed.

“See? Weird.”

Flavio swallowed.

“I’m eating.”

“You stopped.”

“I resumed.”

She stared at him.

“You talk like an old man.”

That almost made him choke.

Teresa finally turned around.

“Leave your brother alone.”

“I’m not doing anything.”

“You’re staring at him.”

“He started it.”

Flavio looked from one to the other and felt something tighten in his chest, not painfully this time, but enough to make him lower his eyes again.

This had happened before.

Maybe not this exact argument, but close enough. Isabel complaining, Teresa answering without looking, Flavio pretending he was not listening because he had schoolwork or somewhere to go.

Nothing important.

That was what made it important now.

He kept eating while Isabel returned to her notebook, muttering under her breath.

Flavio glanced at the page.

There were numbers written in a column, several crossed out, with one problem circled hard enough to leave an impression on the paper.

“What are you doing?”

“Homework.”

“I can see that.”

“Then why did you ask?”

Flavio paused.

She had a point.

He leaned slightly closer.

“What subject?”

Isabel gave him a suspicious look.

“Math.”

“I know it’s math.”

“Then you already know the answer to your question.”

Flavio stared at her.

She had gotten better at this with age, but apparently the foundation had always been there.

Teresa covered a laugh with a cough.

Flavio looked toward her.

“You’re encouraging her.”

“I didn’t say anything.”

“You laughed.”

“I coughed.”

“That was not a cough.”

Teresa turned back to the counter.

“Eat.”

Flavio shook his head, but there was a smile he could not completely hide.

Isabel noticed that too.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“You keep saying nothing.”

“Because you keep asking what.”

She considered this for a moment, then pushed the notebook toward him.

“Fine. If you’re not doing anything, help me.”

Flavio looked down at the page.

The problem was simple.

At least, it looked simple now.

Seventeen-year-old Flavio had probably needed a little longer.

Thirty-five-year-old Flavio had spent years dealing with calculations nobody called mathematics because they involved medicine costs, loan payments, transportation, groceries, repair materials, interest, and how much money could survive until payday.

He picked up Isabel’s pencil.

Before he could write, his eyes landed on a small dent in the wooden handle.

He remembered biting pencils when he was thinking.

Apparently Isabel did too.

His hand stopped.

Another tiny thing.

Another useless detail memory had discarded.

“Kuya?”

Flavio looked up.

“Hmm?”

“You’re holding my pencil.”

“I know.”

“You’ve been holding it for a while.”

“Thinking.”

“The answer is not that hard.”

He looked at her.

“You asked me for help.”

“I didn’t say I was the one who needed it.”

Flavio set the pencil down.

“Do it yourself.”

“What?!”

“You seem confident.”

“That’s not fair!”

“Life is difficult.”

Isabel stared at him in disbelief.

Teresa laughed openly this time.

Flavio leaned back, satisfied.

There.

That felt normal.

Not the impossible kind of normal his mind kept trying to examine, but the ordinary kind that required no explanation.

Isabel grabbed the pencil.

“You’re useless.”

“You asked the useless person.”

“Because I thought you knew.”

“I do.”

“Then tell me!”

“No.”

She made a noise somewhere between a groan and an accusation.

Flavio kept eating.

He had missed this.

Not helping with homework.

The irritation.

The complete lack of gratitude.

Being needed for something that would not ruin anyone’s life if he got it wrong.

Isabel attacked the problem again.

Flavio watched her for a moment, then reached over and tapped one line with his finger.

“Start here.”

She followed where he pointed.

“This?”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Because you skipped a step.”

“I didn’t skip.”

“You did.”

“I combined them.”

“You combined them incorrectly.”

She frowned at the page.

“That’s the same thing.”

“No, it’s more impressive.”

Isabel looked at him, then back at the notebook.

A few seconds later, she erased half the calculation and started over.

Flavio returned to his food.

He did not solve it for her.

Some habits had survived thirty-five years for a reason.

His father had been the same way.

The thought arrived so naturally that Flavio’s hand stopped halfway to the plate.

Rogelio rarely gave direct answers when teaching him something practical. If Flavio held a tool wrong, his father would correct his grip. If he chose the wrong one, Rogelio would sometimes let him struggle until frustration forced him to look again.

Not cruelly.

Patiently.

Annoyingly.

Flavio had hated it.

Later, he had understood why.

His eyes moved toward the small shelf near the back wall.

A pair of old pliers rested there.

He knew them immediately.

The handles were worn, the metal darkened with age, and one side had a tiny chip near the joint.

His father’s.

Flavio stared at them too long.

The kitchen sounds faded around him.

Rogelio had been dead for two years.

That had not changed.

Flavio had already confirmed it in the photograph, but the pliers made the absence feel different. A photograph could show who was missing. An object could show where someone had been.

The tool sat exactly where his father had once left it.

Or perhaps Teresa had moved it there later.

Flavio did not remember.

He wanted to.

That bothered him.

He had spent years believing he remembered Rogelio well because he remembered the important things: his voice, his work, the day he died, the responsibilities that followed.

But standing inside the old house, Flavio began noticing how much of his father had disappeared in smaller pieces.

Where he placed tools. How he sat. Which cup he used. Whether he whistled when he worked or only when he was in a good mood.

Things nobody thought to preserve.

Teresa followed his gaze.

“You’re looking at your father’s pliers.”

Flavio glanced at her.

“Yeah.”

She wiped her hands on a cloth.

“I’ve been meaning to put those away.”

“Why?”

Teresa looked at the pliers.

“They’re just sitting there.”

Flavio almost said leave them.

The words reached his throat and stopped.

It was not his decision.

Not then.

Maybe not now either.

“They’re fine there,” he said instead.

Teresa studied him for a moment.

“If they bother you, I’ll move them.”

“They don’t.”

She nodded once and returned to the counter.

No speech. No shared confession. Just pliers on a shelf.

Flavio was grateful for that.

Isabel suddenly pushed her notebook across the table.

“Done.”

Flavio looked down.

The answer was correct.

“You fixed it.”

“I told you I knew how.”

“You told me you needed help.”

“I changed my mind.”

He handed the notebook back.

“Then I did nothing.”

“You pointed.”

“That was emotional support.”

“That’s not emotional support!”

“It was very emotional.”

Isabel looked at Teresa.

“Ma!”

Teresa sighed.

“Both of you, stop.”

Flavio smiled into his plate.

This house would be lost.

The thought came without warning.

Not today. Not tomorrow. But he remembered the years ahead. The strain. The money. The choices that stopped feeling like choices.

He remembered packing things into boxes and deciding what they could keep. He remembered looking at the walls one last time, already exhausted enough that grief had felt inconvenient.

At the time, he had told himself a house was only a building.

Family mattered more.

He still believed that.

But he had been lying about the other part.

The house had mattered too.

Not because of the roof or the walls.

Because Rogelio’s pliers were still on the shelf. Because Isabel had scratched the table. Because Teresa wrote grocery reminders on the calendar. Because the fan in his room clicked. Because every corner held evidence that their lives had happened here before everything became about surviving what came next.

Flavio looked around the kitchen carefully.

This time, he wanted to remember it correctly.

The small window. The stain near one cabinet hinge. The plastic container Teresa refused to replace. The chair with one uneven leg. The place on the wall where paint had begun to peel.

Nothing grand.

Nothing anyone would photograph unless they were leaving.

He had already left once.

Maybe that was why he could finally see it.

Isabel closed her notebook.

“Why are you looking around like that?”

Flavio turned toward her.

“Like what?”

“Like you’re checking the house.”

He considered lying.

Then decided he did not need a good one.

“I just noticed it.”

Isabel looked around.

“What’s there to notice?”

Flavio smiled.

“Exactly.”

She frowned.

“That makes no sense.”

“It will eventually.”

“You really do sound old.”

Flavio laughed.

“Finish breakfast.”

“I finished before you.”

He looked at his plate.

She had.

That felt unfair.

Teresa placed a glass of water in front of him.

“You’re going to be late if you keep staring at everything.”

Flavio checked the clock and immediately felt the old instinct return.

School. Schedule. Move.

For a second, his body responded before his mind did, shoulders tightening as if being late by a few minutes might begin the same eighteen-year chain that had ended on wet pavement.

He stopped himself.

Not dramatically.

Just enough.

There was time.

He finished the last of his breakfast, drank the water, and stood.

Isabel had already gathered her notebook.

Teresa reached for the plate, but Flavio carried it toward the sink instead.

“I’ll wash it.”

She looked at him.

“You’ll be late.”

“I have time.”

Teresa’s eyebrows rose slightly.

That simple statement seemed to confuse her more than anything else he had done all morning.

Flavio turned on the tap.

Cold water ran over his fingers.

Behind him, Isabel whispered loudly enough for everyone to hear.

“He’s definitely sick.”

Flavio laughed.

“I can hear you.”

“That doesn’t prove you’re not sick.”

He washed the plate while Teresa shook her head.

For the first time since waking, Flavio did not think about Genesis, the future, money, or why he had returned.

He stood in the kitchen of the house he had lost once and washed a breakfast plate while his sister insulted him from behind.

It was enough.

For now, it was more than enough.

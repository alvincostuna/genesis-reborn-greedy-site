E002-P02 — Rules Without Answers

The private objective did not return the next morning.

Flavio checked once after waking, once while washing his face, and once more while tying his shoes, which was already two checks more than he wanted to admit. Nothing appeared. No new instruction floated in front of him, no reward had been waiting overnight, and his body felt exactly as tired as a seventeen-year-old body should after walking farther than it was used to.

That should have been reassuring.

Instead, the silence made him suspicious.

At breakfast, Isabel watched him stare into empty space above his rice.

“What now?”

Flavio blinked.

“What?”

“You keep looking at nothing.”

“I’m thinking.”

“That is what people say when they are looking at something they don’t want to explain.”

Teresa set down a plate.

“Eat before your thinking gets cold.”

Flavio obeyed.

That was the strange thing about impossible events: they did not stop the ordinary ones. Rice still had to be cooked. Teresa still checked whether there was enough money for medicine. Isabel still needed school supplies at the exact moment the household cash was committed elsewhere. The door latch still needed repair.

Rebirth had excellent dramatic value and terrible purchasing power.

Flavio discovered that later that evening while sitting at the kitchen table with three small piles of money in front of him.

Food.

Medicine.

Transport and school.

A fourth category existed only in his imagination.

Everything else.

Isabel leaned over his shoulder.

“Why are you sorting coins like they insulted you?”

“I’m budgeting.”

“You always budget.”

“I’m budgeting differently.”

“That sounds threatening.”

Flavio ignored her and looked at the medicine receipt.

The first-life version of him had known exactly what to do whenever the numbers refused to fit.

Work more.

If the total was wrong, increase income until it behaved.

The method had kept the family alive often enough that nobody questioned the price until Flavio’s body finally sent the bill.

He remembered wet pavement.

A stranger’s hand.

The Genesis screen above the road.

Live more.

Flavio moved one coin from the imaginary everything-else category into food.

Isabel gasped.

Flavio looked up.

“What?”

“You increased the food budget.”

“I corrected an underestimate.”

“That is the same thing wearing different clothes.”

Teresa entered carrying laundry and heard the last part.

“What is happening?”

“Kuya voluntarily gave food more money.”

Teresa stopped.

Flavio rubbed his forehead.

“We need to eat.”

“Yes,” Teresa said. “This is a recent discovery?”

“No.”

“Then why is your sister announcing it like a national event?”

“Because normally he cuts his own portion before he changes a number.”

Flavio’s hand stopped above the table.

Isabel had said it lightly.

The memory beneath it was not light.

He had done that.

Not dramatically. Never enough for anyone to panic. A smaller breakfast because he was not hungry. Coffee instead of lunch because he was busy. Less rice because he had already eaten somewhere else.

Sometimes somewhere else had been fictional.

Teresa set the laundry down.

“Anak.”

“I know.”

“No, you know numbers.” She pulled out a chair. “I’m asking if you know what she said.”

Flavio looked at the coins.

He wanted to defend the choices because at the time they had made sense.

That defense had followed him all the way to the street where he died.

“I know now.”

Teresa studied him for a moment, then tapped the notebook.

“Then put yourself in the budget.”

“I am in the food budget.”

“As a person, not a rounding error.”

Isabel pointed at her mother.

“Write that down.”

Flavio opened his mouth to refuse.

Then he actually wrote it in the margin.

Put yourself in the budget.

Isabel stared.

Teresa laughed.

The sound made Flavio feel strangely embarrassed.

“Happy?”

“Very,” Isabel said.

The impossible interface did not appear.

Flavio was glad.

Some lessons did not need special effects.

Later that afternoon, a man from two streets over brought a broken rice cooker and offered cash if Flavio could return it that evening. The fault looked simple enough after he opened the base: a worn connection near the thermal fuse, not fully burned through yet.

First-life Flavio would have said yes before the man finished asking.

Money now was safer than money later.

At least that was what he had believed.

“What time do you actually need it?” Flavio asked.

The man hesitated.

“Tonight, if possible.”

“Why tonight?”

“For breakfast tomorrow.”

Flavio checked the clock.

Schoolwork waited.

Dinner would come soon.

His legs were still sore from yesterday.

“Tomorrow morning,” he said.

The man blinked.

“You can’t do tonight?”

“I can.”

Flavio heard how strange the next words sounded even before he said them.

“I’m choosing not to.”

The man looked disappointed, but not angry.

“Early?”

“Seven.”

“Fine.”

That was it.

No argument.

No lost customer.

No disaster.

The man left the rice cooker behind and went home.

Flavio stared at the door after him.

His heart was beating faster than the conversation deserved.

Saying no to several hours of possible paid work had frightened him more than an invisible objective counting his steps.

That was an unpleasant discovery.

At dinner, Flavio ate a full portion.

Teresa noticed.

He noticed her noticing.

Neither said anything.

Isabel ruined the silence by spreading school materials across the table.

“I need illustration board.”

“How much?”

She named the price.

“And glue.”

“How much?”

“Kuya.”

“What?”

“I am not submitting a procurement request.”

“You are asking for money.”

“I am asking for glue.”

“Money purchases the glue.”

Teresa did not look up from folding clothes.

“Give your sister money before she graduates while waiting.”

Flavio counted the amount and handed it over.

Isabel held the bills suspiciously.

“No lecture?”

“About what?”

“Keeping receipts.”

“Keep the receipt.”

“There it is.”

Flavio almost smiled.

The household was still poor.

That had not changed because he died.

Money still mattered.

Food still cost money.

Medicine still cost too much.

The difference was that Flavio could now see how quickly necessity became permission to erase himself.

He did not like what he saw.

That evening, Teresa’s medicine organizer had two compartments running low. Flavio checked the packaging, compared prices he remembered, and began calculating whether buying the larger quantity would reduce the cost per dose.

Teresa watched him.

“You’re doing medicine math again.”

“It saves money.”

“Sometimes.”

“It does this time.”

“I’m not arguing with the math.”

Flavio looked up.

Teresa leaned against the counter.

“I’m arguing with the face.”

“What face?”

“The one that says if medicine costs too much, you personally failed.”

Flavio had no immediate response.

That irritated him.

Immediate responses were useful.

Teresa continued before he invented one.

“Medicine is expensive because medicine is expensive. Food costs money because food costs money. Isabel needs things because she is still in school. None of that means you have to punish yourself until the numbers behave.”

“I’m not punishing myself.”

Isabel entered at exactly the wrong moment.

“You skipped lunch yesterday.”

Flavio turned.

“I was walking.”

“You can walk and chew.”

Teresa raised one eyebrow.

Flavio understood he had lost the room.

“I’ll eat lunch.”

“Today,” Teresa said.

“Today.”

“And tomorrow.”

“Yes.”

“And sleep before midnight.”

Flavio frowned.

“Those are separate negotiations.”

“No.”

Isabel sat down.

“Ma has bundled the contract.”

Flavio looked at her.

She grinned.

The next morning, another message appeared.

Not from the private interface.

From a customer.

Broken electric fan. Could Flavio check it immediately? The old reply rose in his mind before he finished reading.

Yes. Bring it over.

His thumb hovered above the keyboard.

The money could matter.

Every instinct built by poverty told him available work had to be captured before someone else took it.

Flavio thought of the rice cooker.

The man had waited.

The job had survived the word tomorrow.

He deleted the first reply and typed another.

Tomorrow morning is fine. I can check it after seven. If you need it tonight, I can recommend someone else.

He stared at the message.

It felt irresponsible.

That was how deep the habit went.

Then he sent it.

The reply arrived two minutes later.

Tomorrow is fine.

Flavio put the phone down.

No lost job.

No catastrophe.

Even if there had been, he thought, the answer might still have been right.

The private interface remained silent through the entire day.

Flavio went to school.

He ate lunch.

He returned home.

He repaired the door latch properly, using the old pliers only where they actually helped. He listened when Teresa described where the door still caught. He checked the work twice.

At night, he opened the private notebook.

He had created pages for the objective, the count, the strange condition-recorded message, and his unanswered questions.

He added another line.

No new objective today.

Then beneath it:

Ordinary life continues.

Flavio looked at the sentence.

It was not technical.

It was not useful in the way he usually preferred.

He left it anyway.

Before sleeping, he checked the household budget one more time.

Food remained funded.

Medicine remained funded.

School needs remained funded.

Transport remained funded.

Income was still tight.

Nothing had magically improved.

But Flavio turned off the light while there were still things left undone.

For once, he allowed tomorrow to keep some of its own work.

E002-P03 — The Body Comes Back

The first month taught Flavio something humiliating.

One thousand five hundred steps had not been training.

It had been an introduction.

The private interface continued appearing often enough that he could no longer pretend his body would improve by accident, but it never gave him anything grand. Some days it asked for distance. Other days it wanted balance, controlled repetition, a safe load carried with proper posture, or a stretch held long enough to become uncomfortable without becoming stupid.

Sometimes it told him to rest.

Flavio distrusted that one the most.

He had spent too many years treating rest like a reward that had to be purchased with exhaustion. An instruction that simply said stop when form breaks felt almost suspiciously generous.

It also worked.

The first time he tried to finish a walking objective faster by jogging, his shin began to ache, his stride shortened, and the counter stopped recognizing part of the effort. Flavio slowed down because there was no point arguing with a number only he could see.

The count resumed.

He tried speeding up again later.

Same result.

That annoyed him.

It also made sense.

By the second month, he could walk farther without thinking about his breathing. By the third, he added short jogs, the kind no one would describe as heroic unless the hero had recently recovered from a long illness.

Nico saw him one afternoon and nearly dropped his drink.

“You exercise now?”

Flavio slowed to a walk.

“I have always moved.”

“That is not exercise. You move because someone is paying you or something is broken.”

“That is inaccurate.”

Nico pointed at him.

“Name one recreational physical activity you did last month.”

Flavio thought about it.

“Walking.”

“Where?”

“To the hardware store.”

“That was an errand!”

“It involved walking.”

Nico pressed a hand to his chest.

“I am witnessing personal growth, and you are trying to invoice it.”

Flavio kept walking.

Nico followed.

The joking disappeared after several steps.

“Seriously,” he said. “You okay?”

Flavio looked at him.

“Yes.”

Nico studied him more carefully.

“You look better.”

That was true.

Not dramatically better.

His shoulders no longer rounded forward as badly. Stairs stopped feeling like a negotiation. He could carry groceries without needing to shift the bag every few minutes. He slept more regularly because every attempt to sacrifice sleep for another repair job now came with the memory of wet pavement and a stranger saying stay with me.

He was still lean.

Still ordinary.

Still capable of getting winded.

Just less fragile.

“I’m taking care of myself,” Flavio said.

Nico stared at him.

“Who are you?”

Flavio laughed.

The fourth month brought balance work.

The fifth brought grip endurance.

That was when Rogelio returned to him through his hands.

Flavio was repairing a loose cabinet latch in the kitchen when a screwdriver slipped. His first instinct was to squeeze harder. The old habit was immediate: if something resisted, add force.

Then a memory moved through him before the thought became complete.

A large hand over his smaller one.

A thumb adjusting the angle.

A voice close enough that he could almost hear the breath behind it.

Don’t fight the tool.

Flavio froze.

The kitchen stayed quiet around him.

Teresa was outside hanging laundry. Isabel had already gone to school. The screwdriver rested in his hand exactly as it had a second before, but Flavio could feel his father’s correction as clearly as if Rogelio were standing behind him.

He loosened his grip.

Changed the angle.

Let the pressure travel through the shaft instead of crushing the handle.

The screw turned.

Flavio stared at it.

He remembered almost nothing else about the day the lesson had happened. Maybe he had been eleven. Maybe twelve. He remembered the smell of oil and metal. He remembered being annoyed because Rogelio refused to let him solve every problem with more strength.

He smiled despite himself.

The private interface did not appear.

Good.

Flavio did not want the moment classified.

He finished the repair and left the memory alone.

By the sixth month, the objectives had become part of his life without becoming the center of it. That mattered.

He went to school.

He repaired small things for neighbors.

He helped Isabel when she asked and, occasionally, when she did not ask.

He ate with Teresa.

He exercised because the interface gave him a structure he could not dismiss as laziness, but he stopped treating every objective like an emergency.

That change took longer than the physical training.

One evening, the display appeared while Flavio was finishing a repair.

[CONDITION]

Rest.

He stared at it.

The fan in front of him still needed one final screw.

Flavio put the screwdriver down.

Teresa noticed immediately.

“You’re done?”

“For now.”

She looked toward the unfinished fan.

“For now?”

“Yes.”

Her eyebrows rose.

“What happened to you?”

“I died.”

The words escaped before Flavio could stop them.

Teresa stared.

Flavio stared back.

Then he coughed.

“Inside. From tiredness.”

She narrowed her eyes.

“That was a terrible joke.”

“Yes.”

“Do not make jokes like that.”

“I won’t.”

Flavio looked away, the humor gone as quickly as it had arrived.

Teresa softened.

“Sit down.”

He did.

She gave him water.

The interface disappeared sometime later.

No reward.

No punishment.

Just rest.

By the seventh and eighth months, the objectives shifted toward reaction and coordination. Catch something dropped from a controlled height. Step through marked spaces without looking directly at his feet. Change direction without crossing his legs badly. Maintain balance while carrying a safe weight.

Flavio failed often.

That helped.

If his heel landed badly, he knew. If he anticipated instead of watching, he moved too early. If he tightened his shoulders before catching something, his hands became clumsy.

The private interface did not explain mistakes.

His body did.

Nico became increasingly suspicious.

“You are getting annoying.”

“I have always been annoying.”

“Different annoying.”

“That seems subjective.”

“You move like you know where your feet are now.”

Flavio looked down.

“They are still attached.”

Nico pointed at him.

“That. That is what I mean. You became healthier and somehow more irritating.”

“Efficient.”

“No.”

By the ninth month, Flavio could repeat movements without wasting as much effort. By the tenth, he stopped checking the mirror for dramatic changes because there were none.

His body looked healthier.

That was all.

Lean shoulders with more shape. Legs that could carry him through a long day without trembling. Hands steadier than before. Posture that no longer apologized to gravity.

Nico remained disappointed by the lack of a secret explanation.

“You cannot just become responsible for ten months and expect me not to investigate.”

“You can choose not to.”

“No. There should be an incident.”

“There was no incident.”

That was technically false.

Flavio felt no need to explain death and reincarnation.

Nico squinted.

“Romance?”

“No.”

“Cult?”

“No.”

“Illegal sports betting?”

Flavio stopped tightening the repaired strap on his bag.

“What kind of training program do you think illegal sports betting provides?”

“I’m brainstorming.”

“You’re failing.”

Nico looked at Flavio’s hands.

“You got faster at that too.”

The strap buckle had bent. Flavio had straightened it, adjusted the worn hole, and reinforced the edge without thinking much about the order.

“Practice.”

“With what?”

Flavio looked around the house.

The fan clicked faintly.

A cabinet hinge squeaked.

A chair by the wall carried one of his old metal reinforcements.

“Everything breaks.”

Nico looked around.

Then nodded.

“Fair.”

The final two months did not make Flavio powerful.

They made him consistent.

He could walk farther, jog without immediately regretting it, lift safely, recover properly, and notice when fatigue changed the way he moved. He could stop before pain became injury.

That last part remained the hardest.

Flavio had once believed persistence meant continuing regardless of cost.

Now he was learning that persistence could also mean returning tomorrow.

The distinction irritated him because it sounded like something wise people said after surviving mistakes.

He had already supplied the mistake.

Genesis launch moved closer.

Advertisements grew larger. Nico talked about system requirements, regional schedules, NPC behavior, persistent economies, and all the things Flavio had ignored in his first life.

Flavio listened now.

Not because he had suddenly become a gamer.

Because the private interface had been using words that sounded increasingly familiar.

Objective.

Condition.

Inventory.

Durability.

Words that belonged to systems.

Words Genesis used publicly too.

He wrote them down.

He did not call that proof.

Not yet.

On the last evening before the final month began, Flavio finished a controlled run around the neighborhood and stopped near the house. His breathing was heavy but steady. Sweat ran down his neck. His legs were tired without shaking.

The private interface appeared.

[CONDITION RECORDED]

Flavio waited.

No explanation followed.

He laughed softly.

“Still refusing to explain yourself?”

Nothing answered.

He looked toward the house.

Teresa was inside.

Isabel was arguing with someone over the phone.

The fan in his room would probably click when he turned it on.

For once, Flavio did not need the interface to tell him what had changed.

His body had come back.

Not the body he had at thirty-five.

Something better.

A body he had finally learned not to spend like money.
